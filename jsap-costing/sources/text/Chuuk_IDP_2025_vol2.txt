Foreword by Governor
Public infrastructure is the backbone of any thriving society,
playing a crucial role in economic growth, social well-being, and
environmental sustainability. This Infrastructure Development
Plan presents our 10-year capital development needs to ensure
our public infrastructure assets meet the current and future
demands from our community. This includes:
      Transportation: Improving road networks, air and seaports,
       to facilitate efficient and safe movement of people and
       goods driving productivity.
      Energy: Upgrading energy grids and promoting renewable
       energy sources to ensure a reliable and sustainable power
       supply.
      Water and Sanitation: Enhancing water supply systems
       and wastewater management to provide clean water and
       maintain public health.
      Public Facilities: Building and renovating schools, hospitals,
       and recreational facilities to improve quality of life and
       community services.
      Marine, Agriculture and Coastal: Protecting our coastlines
       and communities from climate events and ensuring our
       primary economic sectors remain productive.

Infrastructure often has a high cost, requires careful planning and
multi-faceted design. It needs ongoing management and
maintenance to deliver critical services to the public. By investing
in our infrastructure, we aim to create a foundation for long-term
prosperity and a higher quality of life for all residents.

The five volumes of this 2025-2034 IDP were approved and adopted
by the twenty third congress of the Federated States of Micronesia
on 24 January 2025.

Compact of Free Association. Under Compact III the US will provide over $350m of capital development,

partners, including US agencies. The Infrastructure Development Plan (IDP) provides these investment

                                            Multi-Sector, Multi-year Investment Plans
                                            An Infrastructure Development Plan (IDP) examines the
                                            infrastructure needs of all sectors of the nation; be they
                                            economic, social or administrative in nature, drawing on the
                                            existing hierarchy of national, sectoral and institutional level
                                            strategic plans. This brings together a list of candidate
                                            investment projects, which are screened and prioritized in a
                                            process that is both systematic and transparent. At the same
                                            time an assessment is made of likely resource availability from
                                            sources including government, state-owned enterprises,
                                            development partners, and private sector and community
                                            interests, to enable the infrastructure investment program to
                                            be scaled appropriately.


                                            Primary Objectives
                                            The primary aim of a multi-year Infrastructure Development
                                            Plan is to guide efficient government infrastructure investment
                                            that meet the socio-economic development objectives over a
                                            10-year horizon. An IDP allows the identification of clear
                                            priorities for infrastructure investments; it improves the
                                            alignment of expenditures with available resources; and it can
                                            secure better coordination of investments. These efforts in turn
                                            can ensure that the impacts of investments are maximized.
                                            Moreover, in an environment of heavy dependence on aid,
                                            good planning can secure more timely and reliable
                                            commitments of development partner resources.

Concessional Loans
Concessionary terms for loans mean FSM has access to funds for infrastructure at a rate below market
lending rates. Eligibility for concessional loans and grants is dependent on individual development
partner policies and decision-making.

Most infrastructure projects over the last 10 years have been funded by grants or concessional loans,
as FSM is a lower-middle income country. This classification, assigned by the World Bank Group, is
based on the gross national income (GNI) per capita of the previous calendar year. The threshold for
lower-middle income countries is between $1,146 and $4,515 (at 1 July 2024) . FSM had a reported
gross national income per capita of $4,140 in 2022 (WBG, 2024) moving it toward the upper-middle
income band, potentially impacting its level of access to concessional funding from some development
partners.


Financing Infrastructure
The latest debt sustainability analysis for FSM was reported jointly by the World Bank and the
International Monetary Fund in October 2021. It assessed both the exposure and risk of debt stress as
“High”. This rating can reduce FSM’s access to loan funding for infrastructure. Should the multilaterals
reduce their concessional loans or increase interest rates, this will automatically reduce the funding
available for new infrastructure, and its maintenance and operations. Hence, it’s crucial that the
infrastructure projects built in the next 10 to 20 year period increase FSM’s economic independence,
protect its natural resources, its environmental wealth and the health and education of its residents and
citizens.

2023 Amended Compact III Funding Forecast
Under Section 266 of the 2023 Amended Compact, FSM is to receive grant payments of $140 million
per year FY2024 to FY2043. The qualifying sectors include health, education, public infrastructure,
private sector development, the environment, public sector capacity building, and enhancing reporting
and accountability. Sector grants for public infrastructure shall not be less than a three-year moving
average of 25% of the annual grant amount or approximately $35m per year.

Beginning in FY2024, the Infrastructure Maintenance Fund (IMF), pursuant to Section 211 of the
Amended Compact, shall be governed by Article VII of the Fiscal Procedures Agreement 2023 (FPA).
Annual contributions to the IMF start at 7% of the $140 million in FY2024 and increase thereafter
(Table 7). The IMF is for the repair and maintenance of US Funded Infrastructure only and it cannot be
used for expansion or repurposing infrastructure. Funds from the health and education grants may also
be used to fund maintenance requirements in those sectors.

When allocating Compact funds, “Priority should be given to, but not limited to, primary and secondary
education capital projects and projects that directly affect health and safety, including water and
wastewater projects, solid waste disposal projects, healthcare facilities, airport and seaport
improvements, roads, seawalls, and energy development including renewable energy that cannot be
funded through the rate structure.” (FPA, 2023).

Chuuk State’s ten-year indicative Compact III funding allocation for sector grants for public
infrastructure is $133m. In addition to the sector grants for public infrastructure, there are sector grants
for environment which may include “environmental infrastructure planning, design construction and
operation”. The funding allocation for sector grants for environment is unknown.

World Bank
The World Bank’s support to the Federated States of Micronesia (FSM) is focused on addressing a
range of interconnected global challenges. Central to these efforts is the goal of ending extreme
poverty by 2030, boosting shared prosperity, and achieving more equitable societies. Emphasising
inclusion, the World Bank aims to enhance the distribution of opportunities, resources, and choices for
all, particularly for women, youth, and marginalised communities, with a special focus on human
development areas such as education, health, and social protection. Additionally, the World Bank aims
to build resilience, helping people and countries prevent, prepare for, and recover from shocks like
climate and biodiversity crises, pandemics, and conflicts. Sustainability is another vital theme, with
actions directed towards promoting economic growth, job creation, macroeconomic stability, prudent
fiscal and debt management, food security, and access to clean air, water, and affordable energy.


US Agency for International Development (USAID)
USAID’s programs in the FSM address environment and climate change, disaster preparedness,
fisheries, health, and democracy and governance, working closely with government organisations,
NGOs, multilateral agencies, and the private sector to achieve shared development goals. In its 2022-
2027 Strategic Framework for the Pacific Islands, USID identified three key Development Objectives,
with a particular emphasis on Resilience.

These are firstly Community Resilience Strengthened, which focuses on increasing resilience by
adapting to climate change, sustainably managing natural resources, and improving access to clean,
sustainable energy. This also includes strengthening health systems to improve COVID-19 response
and enhance the delivery of health care to withstand future shocks. Secondly, Resilient Economic
Growth Advanced, which promotes post-COVID economic recovery through sustainable development
practises. Thirdly, Democratic Governance Strengthened, which aims to enhance democracy, human
rights, and governance, and support conflict mitigation in fragile areas.


Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade, Australia (DFAT)
Australia’s priorities within FSM involve supporting education, the environment and oceans, COVID-19
recovery, and women's social and economic empowerment. Australia also focuses on enhancing
regional connectivity, linking the North and Central Pacific to each other and Australia. Looking forward,
DFAT places an emphasis on projects that aim to meet genuine needs and deliver long-term benefits.

DFAT aligns its efforts with several key Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) including:
▪ SDG 3 – Good health and well-being
▪ SDG 4 – Quality education
▪ SDG 5 – Gender equality
▪ SDG 6 – Clean water and sanitation
▪ SDG 8 – Decent work and economic growth
▪ SDG 9 – Industry, innovation, and infrastructure
▪ SDG 14 – Life below water
▪ SDG 16 – Peace, justice and strong institutions

Green Climate Fund (GCF)
The GCF is the largest climate fund in the world and has the largest capacity building program for
climate finance access. The FSM-GCF partnership began in 2016 to pursue a transformational climate
action agenda that would see FSM meeting their international contributions to the United Nations
Framework Convention on Climate Change sustainable development and climate goals. The Secretary
of the FSM Department of Finance & Administration is the FSM’s National Designated Authority to the
GCF. In recognizing the greater impact of a paradigm-level and systemic approach to address the
double challenge of sustainable development and climate change, the FSM has developed a robust
Country Program with the Fund. The Country Program is underpinned by the FSM’s Sustainable
Development Strategic Framework and Vision of ‘safe and resilient communities prospering in a low
carbon economy’. The framework not only aligns with FSM’s Nationally Determined Contributions and
the SDGs, but importantly underscores the global significance of the FSM as a ‘Large Ocean State in
the Blue Pacific Continent’, making the FSM one of the few places on earth uniquely positioned to
protect, restore and expand natural carbon-sequestering ecosystems.

As ‘climate custodians’, FSM’s partnership with the GCF is of great importance as it supports FSM
communities to carve out a development pathway built not only with quality, resilient infrastructure but
also one that can thrive within a country-wide infrastructure system built for resilience. The FSM-GCF
Country Program (2024-2027) pipeline of projects are infrastructure in nature and are being developed
through a prioritization process that draws from the endorsed priorities listed in the IDPs, the Joint State
Action Plans and the ODA Priorities.


Japan International Cooperation Agency (JICA)
Under its country assistance policy for the Federated States of Micronesia (FSM), the Japan
International Cooperation Agency (JICA) focuses on two main priorities. The first priority is overcoming
vulnerability, which involves supporting infrastructure development, particularly in maritime affairs, as
well as improving health and education, with a focus on agriculture and fisheries. The second priority is
promoting sustainable economic growth, which aims to enhance economic infrastructure and improve
the business environment to stimulate economic activities and development.


China
The Economic and Technical Cooperation Agreement is China’s main instrument for providing bilateral
grant aid to the FSM. This grant assistance plays an important role in promoting FSM’s economic and
social development. China’s framework emphasizes long-term cooperation in various sectors including
infrastructure, livelihoods and emergency humanitarian assistance.

d) Water Security – Water security relates to ensuring continued access to sufficient quantities of
   acceptable quality water for sustaining livelihoods, human well-being, and socioeconomic
   development, while managing water-related risks. In infrastructure projects, this means designing
   systems that sustainably manage water resources, prevent scarcity, and mitigate disaster risks.
   Infrastructure examples include building water storage, rainwater harvesting, and implementing
   water efficient technologies. Water security in the FSM is likely to become an increasingly difficult
   challenge in the context of climate change, which will negatively impact the quality and quantity of
   both surface freshwater and groundwater.
e) Sanitation and Solid Waste – Sanitation involves providing facilities and services for the safe
   management of human waste to prevent disease and ensure environmental hygiene. Infrastructure
   examples include sewage treatment plants, sewer lift stations and effluent disposal systems.
   Sanitation infrastructure projects help to protect public health, reduce waterborne diseases, and
   support sustainable development, fostering healthier communities and a cleaner environment.
   Solid waste management is one of the most pressing environmental, social and economic issues
   facing sustainable development in FSM. FSM increasingly relies on imported consumer goods
   packed with plastic materials and waste, and there is an urgent need to develop self-sustaining
   waste management systems, including waste minimization, safe and sanitary public landfills,
   recycling and compost facilities.
f)   Public Health – This involves initiatives that support the health and welfare of the population.
     Infrastructure examples include the building and upgrading of healthcare facilities and improving
     access to medical services through purchasing a medical support vessel. These efforts intersect
     with water supply and sanitation infrastructure as improving water quality and sanitation will have
     direct benefits on the quality of life, and reduce the burden on health services.
g) Education – This involves initiatives that are aimed at improving access to quality education for all.
   In the context of infrastructure, this includes the construction or renovation for educational
   infrastructure, providing essential utilities such as electricity, clean water, and sanitation facilities, as
   well as developing reliable transportation networks to enable students to safely and easily travel to
   and from educational institutions. These efforts collectively create a robust educational
   environment that supports effective learning and development.
h) Social Protection and Food Security – Social protection programs provide benefits to individuals on
   the basis of risks faced across the life cycle (e.g., unemployment, disability, maternity, etc.) and to
   those suffering general poverty and social exclusion. Climate change will continue to increase the
   risks people face throughout their lives, directly increasing poverty, vulnerability and inequality.
   Social protection is widely recognized as an essential strategy to help to stabilize the economy and
   protect vulnerable groups and infrastructure projects that enhance food security will assist this.
i)   Economic Development– Economic development involves creating conditions that support
     economic growth, job creation, and improved living standards. Infrastructure project examples
     include developing agricultural and fisheries facilities, improving transportation links, and building
     public markets. By incorporating economic development principles, projects can attract
     investment, stimulate local economies, and provide employment opportunities, leading to overall
     socio-economic progress and enhanced quality of life.
j)   Governance – Strengthening democratic institutions and processes to enhance democracy,
     human rights and governance. This ensures that governance frameworks support the efficient
     planning, management, and implementation of infrastructure projects.
